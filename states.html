Geography

A land of extremes

The world's highest mountains sit a few thousand kilometres from tropical coastline, desert, and rainforest — all inside one country.

Himalayas, India — Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA

North

The Himalaya

India's northern border is drawn by the youngest and tallest mountain range on the planet. The Himalaya feed most of the country's major rivers, including the Ganges and the Brahmaputra, and are home to states like Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand, and Sikkim, where terraced villages sit at altitudes most countries can't build a road to.

Further south, the land drops into the Indo-Gangetic Plain — one of the most densely farmed and densely populated stretches of land anywhere, built on centuries of river silt.

South

Backwaters & coastline

India has over 7,500 kilometres of coastline. In the far south, Kerala's backwaters — a network of lagoons and canals running parallel to the Arabian Sea — are worked by houseboats and fishing families the way canals are worked in Venice, just slower, and greener.

The Deccan Plateau fills most of the peninsula between the coasts, a dry volcanic tableland that shaped very different farming, architecture, and language traditions from the north.

West

The desert states

Rajasthan, India's largest state by area, holds the Thar Desert along with some of the country's most recognisable architecture — pink-sandstone forts, stepwells, and palaces like Jaipur's Hawa Mahal, built with hundreds of small windows so the royal women could watch the street below without being seen themselves.

28 states, 8 union territories

Every state, a different country

India's states were largely drawn along linguistic lines, which is why crossing a state border can mean a new language, script, cuisine, and festival calendar — sometimes within the same afternoon of driving.
