Cuisine

Built on spice and region

There is no single "Indian food" — there are dozens of regional cuisines, each shaped by climate, trade routes, and what grows nearby.

Spice market, Bangalore — Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA

The pantry

Spice, not just heat

Turmeric, cumin, coriander, mustard seed, cardamom, and chilli form the backbone of most Indian kitchens — but the combinations, called masalas, differ from state to state and even household to household. Spice in Indian cooking is less about heat and more about layering flavour: whole spices bloomed in hot oil, ground spices added later, and fresh herbs finishing the dish.

India has traded spices for over 2,000 years; the search for a direct sea route to its pepper and cinnamon is part of what brought European ships to its coast in the first place.

On the plate

The thali

A thali — literally "plate" — is the everyday way a full Indian meal is served: a central helping of rice or bread surrounded by small bowls of lentils (dal), vegetables, yogurt, pickle, and something sweet. The exact combination changes completely by region: a Gujarati thali leans sweet, a Bengali thali is built around fish and rice, and a Punjabi thali centres on wheat bread and rich lentils.

A regional map

Four kitchens, four countries

North

Wheat, dairy, and tandoor

Wheat breads, butter and cream-based gravies, and the clay tandoor oven define Punjab and the Gangetic plains — think naan, butter chicken, and paneer.

South

Rice, lentil, and coconut

Rice and fermented lentil batters (dosa, idli) dominate, with coconut, curry leaf, and tamarind giving Tamil, Keralan, and Andhra food its sharp, tangy character.

East

Mustard, fish, and sweets

Bengal and Odisha favour mustard oil and river fish, alongside a milk-based sweet-making tradition — rasgulla and sandesh among the best known exports.

West

Sweet, sour, and street food

Gujarat and Maharashtra balance sweet and sour in the same dish, and gave the country much of its street-food culture — pav bhaji, vada pav, and dhokla.
