Culture

A festival for every season

Religion, harvest, and history are woven together so tightly that most Indian festivals celebrate all three at once.

The calendar

Major festivals

Spring

Holi

The festival of colour, celebrated by throwing coloured powder and water, marking the arrival of spring and the triumph of good over evil.

Autumn

Diwali

The festival of lights, when homes are lit with oil lamps and fairy lights to mark Lord Rama's return from exile, and the new financial year for many businesses.

Autumn

Durga Puja & Navratri

Nine nights honouring the goddess Durga, celebrated differently across regions — elaborate pandals in Kolkata, garba dancing in Gujarat.

Winter

Pongal & Makar Sankranti

Harvest festivals marking the sun's move northward, celebrated with kite-flying, sugarcane, and freshly harvested rice across the south and north alike.

Year-round

Eid al-Fitr & Eid al-Adha

Marking the end of Ramadan and the spirit of sacrifice, celebrated by India's large Muslim community with prayers, feasts, and family visits.

Winter

Christmas & Republic Day

Christmas is widely celebrated, especially in Goa and the Northeast; Republic Day on 26 January marks the constitution coming into force in 1950.

Performing arts

Classical dance

India recognises eight classical dance forms, each tied to a specific region and centuries of codified technique — facial expression, hand gestures (mudras), and rhythm are as precisely defined as any Western ballet vocabulary.

Bharatanatyam, from Tamil Nadu, is the oldest of these and the most widely performed today, built from a repertoire originally developed in Hindu temples. Others include Kathak (north India), Odissi (Odisha), and Kathakali (Kerala), instantly recognisable by its elaborate painted masks.

Unity in diversity isn't a slogan here — it's the only way to describe a country with over 19,500 recorded languages and dialects sharing one flag.
